How the Slicing Method Works

A sphere is a perfectly symmetrical three-dimensional shape. By sliding the Slice Height slider ($h$), we cut through the sphere horizontally. The resulting intersection is always a perfect flat circle, called a cross-section.

By studying how the cross-section changes shape as it moves from the center to the poles of the sphere, we can build a physical and intuitive model for why the sphere volume matches the final formula of $V = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3$.

When $h = 0$ (The Equator)

The cross-sectional plane slices directly through the center of the sphere. The radius of the slice ($r_s$) reaches its maximum value, exactly equal to the sphere's primary radius ($r$). The slice area is simply $\pi r^2$.

When $h = r$ (The Pole)

The slicing plane barely touches the top of the sphere. The radius of the slice is reduced to exactly $0$, collapsing the cross-sectional circle into a single infinitesimal point with an area of $0$.

Cavalieri's Principle: Proof Without Calculus

First discovered by ancient mathematicians, Cavalieri's Principle states that if two 3D solids have equal heights, and their parallel cross-sections at every height have equal areas, then the two solids must have the exact same total volume.

We compare a Hemisphere of radius $r$ side-by-side with a Cylinder of radius $r$ and height $r$, from which we have removed an inverted Cone of radius $r$ and height $r$.

Mathematical Proof of Slices at Height $h$:

  • Area of Hemisphere Slice: Since $r_s = \sqrt{r^2 - h^2}$, the circular area is: $A_{\text{sphere slice}} = \pi (r^2 - h^2)$
  • Area of Cylinder minus Cone Slice (Washer): The outer boundary is a cylinder (constant radius $r$, area $\pi r^2$). The inner cutout cone has a $45^\circ$ slope, meaning its radius at height $h$ is exactly $h$ (cone area is $\pi h^2$). Subtracting them gives a circular washer of area: $A_{\text{washer}} = A_{\text{cylinder}} - A_{\text{cone}} = \pi r^2 - \pi h^2 = \pi(r^2 - h^2)$

Since the slice areas are mathematically identical at every single height $h$, the total volume of the hemisphere equals the volume of the cylinder minus the cone: $$V_{\text{hemisphere}} = V_{\text{cylinder}} - V_{\text{cone}} = \pi r^2 (r) - \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 (r) = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$$ Doubling this gives the total sphere volume: $$V_{\text{sphere}} = 2 \times \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$$

Calculus Proof: Integration of Slabs

With calculus, we can model the volume of a sphere as the sum of infinitely many cylindrical "slices" or "disks" of differential height $dh$ stacked from bottom to top (from $h = -r$ to $h = r$).

The volume of each tiny cylindrical disk at height $h$ is its base area times its thickness: $$dV = A_{\text{slice}} \cdot dh = \pi r_s^2 \, dh = \pi(r^2 - h^2) \, dh$$

Definite Integral:

We integrate from $h = -r$ to $h = r$: $$V_{\text{total}} = \int_{-r}^{r} \pi(r^2 - h^2) \, dh$$

Using symmetry, we can calculate from $0$ to $r$ and double the result: $$V_{\text{total}} = 2 \int_{0}^{r} \pi(r^2 - h^2) \, dh$$ $$V_{\text{total}} = 2\pi \left[ r^2 h - \frac{h^3}{3} \right]_{0}^{r}$$ $$V_{\text{total}} = 2\pi \left( \left( r^3 - \frac{r^3}{3} \right) - 0 \right)$$ $$V_{\text{total}} = 2\pi \left( \frac{2}{3}r^3 \right) = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$$

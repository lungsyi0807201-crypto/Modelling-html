What Is an Unknown?

Imagine you are celebrating a classmate's birthday, and someone walks into the room carrying a beautifully wrapped gift box. You know for a fact that there is a real, awesome present inside—maybe a cool gadget or a pack of trading cards.

"Because the lid is closed, you do not yet know what it is until you open it."

In mathematics, letters like $x$, $y$, or $a$ are exactly like that wrapped gift box. They are simply empty boxes used to hold numbers. They represent a value that truly exists, but one that is currently hiding under the wrapping paper. We call these hidden numbers unknowns!

Why Do We Use Unknowns?

Let’s explore the snack shop adventure. You buy a delicious bottle of juice for $10 and a mystery bag of chips whose price is not marked. The cashier tells you your total is $25. How do we represent and solve this easily?

Advantage 1

Convenience in Expression

Without algebra, you'd have to write down this chunky, wordy equation just to explain your math:

"$10 + \text{that price of the potato chips I do not know yet} = $25$"

But using our secret agent letter $x$ instead of that long sentence makes it look clean, compact, and beautiful:

Mathematical Expression
$$10 + x = 25$$
Advantage 2

Helping to Solve Problems

An unknown acts as a bridge. By first treating $x$ as a "known friend" and writing it directly in the equation, you can logically work backwards to unmask its identity.
